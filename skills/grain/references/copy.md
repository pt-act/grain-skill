# Copy

How to write the words for a page that will be set large. Read before step 2 (the argument) and step 5 (the copy deck).

## §1 The argument

Before designing, write the page as a list of claims. One line per section. Each claim states what the section proves, not what it contains.

```
Claim — the proof it carries
```

Bad: `Pricing — what it costs.` (a description of furniture)
Good: `The evening is a fixed menu at 65€, three courses, no choices — the room seats forty, so the kitchen cooks once.` (a claim with its evidence)

Every claim needs proof that exists. If you cannot write the proof in one line, the section has no business being on the page yet. Delete the section, or go find the proof.

The order of the claims is the order of the page. The order should answer the visitor's questions in the order they ask them: what this is → why it is like this → the proof → the price → how to act. Any section that cannot be placed in that sequence is decorative; cut it.

## §2 The two-line arithmetic

A headline must fit two lines at every width, including 390px on a phone. Do this arithmetic before committing to a sentence:

1. Set the headline at the planned desktop size (start at 64px, then adjust).
2. Measure its width. A rough rule: average character width is about 0.5em for a grotesk at display size, slightly more for a serif. At 64px, that is about 32px per character.
3. A two-line headline at 64px can carry roughly `measure / 32` characters per line. On a 1440px page with a 960px measure, that is about 30 characters a line — 60 characters total.
4. On a phone at 390px with a 340px measure and the headline scaled to 44px (about 22px per character), the same headline gets about 15 characters per line.
5. **The phone is the constraint.** A sentence of 60 characters will break into four lines at 44px. Rewrite until it fits two lines at the phone width, then verify the desktop still feels confident.

The working rule that survives this arithmetic: **about 32 characters is safe everywhere at a confident size.** The strongest pages' headlines are short — often five or six words. Long headlines are not deep; they are undecided.

## §3 Sentences and statements

One supporting sentence under the headline. It names the thing and the one thing about it that matters.

A quotation or intro sentence may be set large (statement size, up to 36px on desktop, 24px on a phone) but it is a paragraph, not a headline:

- It is marked `<p>` or `<blockquote>`, never an `<h1>`–`<h6>`.
- It runs to about 25 words, three lines at the most.
- If it needs four lines at statement size, it is secretly a headline. Either make it a headline (and cut to two lines) or make it text (and set it at text size).

Reviewers read a 42px sentence over four lines as a headline that broke the two-line rule. The inspector measures this and warns.

## §4 The full content

Every section delivers its information completely. This is the most common gap between a clean page and a winning one:

- **The menu** — every dish, every price, every section of it. Not a selection.
- **The rooms** — each room with its own name, its own details, its own rate.
- **The event** — every speaker and talk, the full running order.
- **The price** — what it includes, what it does not, how it is paid.
- **The FAQ** — an answer for each real question, not the five easy ones.

Where the brief leaves gaps (dish names, room names, project titles), sample details are expected so the design is seen at full density. Mark them in the report for the client to confirm. Never invent proof: no customers, quotes, reviews, press quotes, logos, or business statistics that were not given.

## §5 The first screen

The first screen answers the visitor's first question in large type:

- An event — what it is, when, where. The dates belong in the headline or at the second size, never only in small text further down.
- A product — what it is and what it costs or does.
- A person — who they are and what they do.
- A place — what it is and where it stands.

Then one supporting sentence. Then one primary action, named for what it does ("Reserve a table", "Read the menu", "Book the tour"), as a real button.

## §6 Voice

Write in the subject's voice, not the web's. A roastery is not "your premier destination for artisanal coffee". The subject's own words — from its signs, its labels, its correspondence — are the copy deck's raw material. If the subject's voice is unknown, write plainly and concretely: nouns and verbs, present tense, no adjectives that could be deleted without loss.
